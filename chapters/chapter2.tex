\chapter{LITERATURE REVIEW}
\label{ch:literature}

\section{Introduction}

This chapter reviews the three bodies of work on which the thesis draws. These are
i) the materials-science literature on electrospinning and bead formation, which
defines what is measured and why, ii) the image-analysis literature on fibrous
microscopy, which describes the way in which the measurement is currently made, and
iii) the computer-vision literature on segmentation, from which the methods used
here are taken. The gap between them is stated at the end of the chapter.

\section{Theoretical Background}

\subsection{Electrospinning and the Formation of Bead Defects}

In electrospinning, a polymer solution is fed through a spinneret held at a high
potential relative to a collector. Above a threshold voltage the electrostatic
repulsion of the surface charge overcomes the surface tension of the pendant
droplet, which deforms into a Taylor cone and emits a charged jet. The jet
undergoes a bending instability and stretches by orders of magnitude while the
solvent evaporates, leaving a solid fibre deposited as a randomly oriented
non-woven mat \cite{doshi1995electrospinning, huang2003review}. A detailed account
of the jet dynamics is given by Reneker and Yarin
\cite{reneker2008electrospinning}.

The morphology of the deposit is determined by a balance of forces, where surface
tension favours the break-up of a cylindrical thread into droplets while
viscoelastic stress and charge repulsion oppose it. At low viscosity, low charge
density or high surface tension the instability is not fully suppressed and the
jet collapses partially, producing ellipsoidal swellings along the fibres. Fong,
Chun and Reneker \cite{fong1999beaded} established the standard account, showing
that higher viscosity and higher net charge density result in fewer and more
elongated beads, whereas higher surface tension has the opposite effect. Bhardwaj
and Kundu \cite{bhardwaj2010electrospinning} review how concentration,
conductivity, voltage, flow rate and tip-to-collector distance interact in
practice.

Since bead content responds so directly to these parameters, bead count and size
are used as the diagnostic output of a parameter sweep, and a recipe is refined
until the mat is bead-free or the bead population acceptably small. Beads also
matter physically, as they reduce the specific surface area that makes these mats
useful in filtration and catalysis, perturb the pore-size distribution that
governs permeability and act as stress concentrators that lower tensile strength.

\subsection{Scanning Electron Microscopy of Nanofibre Mats}

SEM is the standard characterisation tool for these materials. It resolves features
well below the diffraction limit of light microscopy and produces images in which
the contrast derives largely from surface topography, making it suitable for the
assessment of fibre morphology. Micrographs are typically acquired at several
magnifications, a low one to sample enough fibres for a representative
measurement and a higher one to resolve individual features. The
instrument software burns an information banner into the image, in which the scale
bar, the accelerating voltage, the working distance and the magnification are
recorded. The banner is convenient for a human reader and provides the means by
which pixel measurements are converted to physical units. It is not, however, part
of the specimen, and therefore it has to be excluded before any automated analysis.

\subsection{Semantic and Instance Segmentation}

Semantic segmentation assigns a class label to every pixel, whereas instance
segmentation additionally separates the pixels belonging to distinct objects of the
same class. This distinction is decisive in the present task. A semantic model that
labels every bead pixel correctly still produces a single connected component for a
cluster of touching beads, so the derived count is too low while the derived
size distribution contains a spurious large object. Since the laboratory endpoint
is a count and a size distribution rather than an area fraction, an instance-level
output is required.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Classical Image Analysis for Fibrous Microscopy}

The established approach in the materials literature is a sequence of
hand-designed operations: contrast normalisation, thresholding, morphological
cleaning and a splitting step. Otsu's method \cite{otsu1979threshold} selects a
global threshold by maximising the between-class variance and remains the default
in tools such as Fiji \cite{schindelin2012fiji}, while touching objects are
separated by the watershed transform \cite{vincent1991watersheds}, usually applied
to the distance transform of the binary mask. For electrospun materials in
particular, DiameterJ \cite{hotaling2015diameterj} provides a validated
open-source pipeline for fibre diameter and porosity and is widely used, although
its scope is the fibre network rather than discrete defects.

These pipelines are transparent, require no training data and have interpretable
failure modes, but they depend on the foreground being separable by intensity. In
micrographs of a dense fibre mat this does not hold, as bright fibres cross the
field at every angle and occupy the same grey levels as the bead surfaces.
Watershed splitting also has a well-known tendency to over-segment on noisy
distance transforms, which is the failure that corrupts a count most directly.

\subsection{Encoder--Decoder Networks for Semantic Segmentation}

U-Net \cite{ronneberger2015unet} introduced the encoder--decoder architecture with
skip connections that has dominated biomedical segmentation since then. The
contracting path captures context while the expanding path recovers spatial
resolution, and the skip connections restore the high-frequency detail that is lost
to pooling. The architecture was designed explicitly for the regime of interest
here, having been demonstrated on training sets of a few dozen annotated images
together with heavy augmentation. Falk et al.\ \cite{falk2019unet} later packaged
it for cell counting and morphometry, thus confirming that it transfers to
quantification tasks.

U-Net nevertheless remains a semantic model. The standard remedy for instance
separation, which consists in predicting object boundaries as an auxiliary class and
using them to split the foreground, is effective but indirect, as its accuracy
depends on how reliably thin boundaries can be predicted.

\subsection{Instance Segmentation}

Faster R-CNN \cite{ren2015faster} established the two-stage detection paradigm, in
which a region proposal network scores candidate boxes and a second stage
classifies and refines them. Mask R-CNN \cite{he2017mask} extends it with a
parallel branch predicting a binary mask inside each proposal, and replaces the
coarse RoI pooling with RoIAlign to preserve spatial correspondence. Since each
object is handled inside its own region, touching objects are separated by
construction rather than by post-processing, which makes the architecture a
natural choice for counting tasks. Feature pyramid networks \cite{lin2017feature}
supply the multi-scale representation that allows a single model to handle objects
of very different apparent size, directly relevant to a dataset spanning a sixfold
magnification range.

Several methods developed for microscopy exploit the shape regularity of the
targets. StarDist \cite{schmidt2018stardist} represents each object as a
star-convex polygon, well matched to roundish convex objects, and Cellpose
\cite{stringer2021cellpose} learns spatial gradient flows towards object centres
and generalises across cell types without retraining. Bead defects are
approximately ellipsoidal and convex, so these shape priors are a plausible fit
and are noted here as alternatives.

\subsection{One-Stage Detection}
\label{sec:onestage}

One-stage detectors remove the proposal step and predict class and box directly on
a dense grid of locations, which is what makes them fast. The formulation was
introduced by YOLO \cite{redmon2016yolo}, whose principal early weakness was
accuracy on the objects falling between grid cells, that is small and clustered
ones. Lin et al.\ \cite{lin2020focal} identified the cause as the class imbalance
of dense prediction, where the overwhelming majority of candidate locations are
background, and addressed it with a loss that down-weights easy negatives. The
modern YOLO releases \cite{jocher2023ultralytics} keep the one-stage structure but
drop the anchor boxes, adding an optional branch that emits a mask per detection,
which brings the family into the same comparison as the region-based models.

The reason for testing such a model here is not speed but the different inductive
bias. A region-based model reasons about one object at a time inside its own
proposal, whereas a one-stage model reasons about the whole frame at once and must
resolve neighbouring objects through non-maximum suppression rather than by
construction. For a dataset in which the objects touch this is a substantive
difference, and Chapter~\ref{ch:results} measures in which direction it operates.

Two cautions carry into how the result should be read. The weakness of this family
on small objects is documented rather than incidental, and
Section~\ref{sec:small_objects} quantifies it. And a comparison against a
region-based model varies the backbone, the label assignment, the loss and the
augmentation schedule at once, so it answers whether a different family performs
better on this data but not which component is responsible.

\subsection{Detection of Small Objects}
\label{sec:small_objects}

The objects counted here are small in the precise sense used by the COCO
benchmark, where an object is \emph{small} when its area falls below
$32 \times 32 = 1024$ pixels. Under that convention 82.8\% of the particles
annotated here are small, rising to 97\% in the $500\times$ micrographs, and only
1.2\% are large (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}). This determines which published
results are a fair point of comparison, since detector accuracy degrades sharply
and systematically with object size, a decline documented across architectures in
the survey of Liu et al.\ \cite{liu2021survey} and attributed there to the few
pixels each object contributes, the loss of that signal through downsampling, and
the coarse alignment between small objects and the anchors meant to capture them.

Table~\ref{tab:coco_size} holds model and dataset fixed and splits the score by
object size alone, for five architectures on COCO, every entry taken from the
paper that introduced the method. The same pattern holds for all of them: the
small-object score lies between a seventh and a half of the large-object score.
The ratio separates the architectures in a way the pooled AP does not, since the
single-stage detectors of that generation lose the most, YOLOv2 recovering only a
seventh of its large-object accuracy, whereas the two-stage detectors built on a
feature pyramid lose the least. This is the empirical basis for preferring a
pyramid-based two-stage model here.

A second body of work studies domains in which nearly every object is small, and
these absolute numbers set a realistic expectation for the present task.
Table~\ref{tab:small_object_lit} collects the studies whose stated aim is the
detection or segmentation of small objects, together with the figure reported in
this thesis, so the two can be read on the same scale.

Cheng et al.\ \cite{cheng2023soda} give the plainest statement of the difficulty:
DyHead reaches 28.3 AP on small objects on COCO test-dev against 50.3 on medium
and 57.5 on large. The mechanism is geometric and is quantified in the same paper,
where a six-pixel displacement of a predicted box reduces the IoU of a small
object from 100\% to 32.5\% while leaving a medium or large object at 56.6\% or
71.8\%. A localisation error negligible for a large object is fatal for a small
one.

The dedicated benchmarks confirm this in absolute terms. On SODA-D, whose objects
average 20.3 pixels, a standard Faster R-CNN reaches 28.9 AP
\cite{cheng2023soda}. The aerial benchmark AI-TOD-v2 \cite{xu2022tiny} is more
severe still, its objects averaging 12.7 pixels, comparable to the 14.3-pixel
median of the $500\times$ micrographs here, where a standard Faster R-CNN reaches
only 12.8 AP and scores 0.0 on the very-tiny band of 2--8 pixels; even the method
proposed in that paper reaches 24.7 AP. TinyPerson \cite{yu2020tinyperson}
identifies the scale mismatch between pre-training and target data as the cause of
the loss, which is the argument the magnified-input experiment of
Section~\ref{sec:upsample_results} tests directly.

Microscopy studies report far higher scores, although on a materially easier
problem, since both \cite{monchot2021titanium} and \cite{bals2023nanoparticles}
image particles against a clean, uniform substrate rather than through an
occluding fibre network, and both report region-overlap measures rather than an
average precision that must also assign every instance correctly.

The last row is why the table sits here rather than in the results chapter. The
$\mathrm{AP}_{50}$ of 29.8 obtained in this work on masks lies within a tenth of a
point of what a standard Faster R-CNN achieves with boxes on AI-TOD-v2, a
benchmark built for tiny-object detection whose mean object size is within two
pixels of the median here. This is not a claim of equivalence, since the datasets,
metrics and task all differ, but it locates the result: a figure that looks like a
failure against pooled COCO numbers is ordinary against the literature that shares
this object size. Two lessons follow. A feature pyramid is not optional at this
object size, since it is how small objects retain a representation at a resolution
where they still occupy several feature-map cells. And an average precision in the
range reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} must be read against
Tables~\ref{tab:coco_size} and \ref{tab:small_object_lit} rather than against
pooled COCO figures, which are dominated by objects an order of magnitude larger.

\subsection{Prior Work at Epoka University}
\label{sec:epoka_prior}

The problem addressed here has a direct antecedent in the research of the host
institution, which for several years has concerned the counting and segmentation
of small, low-contrast objects in microscopy. The modality differs, being
unstained brightfield rather than SEM, but the difficulties are those that recur
throughout this thesis: objects that touch, backgrounds that are not uniform, and
too few annotated images to train from scratch.

Uka et al.\ \cite{uka2020faster} applied Faster R-CNN to counting cells in
low-contrast micrographs, motivated by the observation that manual counting does
not scale and that unstained acquisition makes the objects harder to separate from
the background; that combination of a counting endpoint with a detector isolating
each object in its own region is the argument made in
Section~\ref{sec:small_objects} for the present work. The same group has
established that preprocessing changes materially what a network can learn: Uka et
al.\ \cite{uka2020preprocessing} report its effect on classifying microscopy
images suffering from non-uniform illumination and low contrast, and
Figure~\ref{fig:contrast} shows the present dataset has the same character, which
is why preprocessing is treated in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} as an experimental
variable.

A subsequent line covers segmentation. Uka et al.\ \cite{uka2021basis} set out the
image-analysis basis for evaluating cell--biomaterial interaction, while Polisi
Duro et al.\ \cite{polisi2024detection} apply U-Net architectures to detection,
segmentation, counting and average-area estimation, the same four endpoints
reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. More recent work
\cite{polisi2023pruning, polisi2025efficient} examines how far such models can be
compressed by pruning and quantisation and how optimiser and loss function affect
segmentation quality, while Polisi Duro et al.\ \cite{polisiduro2025optimizing}
pair three pretrained feature extractors with six classifiers for cell-type
classification. Table~\ref{tab:epoka_results} summarises the headline result of
each where it could be obtained.

One number in that table requires care, since it is the one a reader will reach
for first. The segmentation work of this group reports an intersection over union
between 0.90 and 0.93, against the $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$ of 0.298 reported in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. The two are not on the same scale and cannot be
subtracted. An IoU of that kind is a \emph{pixel-level} measure over a foreground
occupying a large fraction of the frame: it asks how well the predicted region
overlaps the true region, and is indifferent to whether two touching objects were
separated. Average precision is an \emph{instance-level} measure, where every
object must be found, delineated and matched one-to-one, and every spurious
detection counts against the score. A model can hold an IoU near 0.9 while merging
half the objects it finds, which is exactly the failure the U-Net baseline of
Section~\ref{sec:comparison} exhibits.

Measured on the present dataset in that group's own metric, the same U-Net
architecture reaches a pixel-level DICE of 0.667 and an IoU of 0.501, and Mask
R-CNN 0.625 and 0.454. The gap against 0.90 is therefore not between this work and
that one, but between cells that are large, dense and imaged against a controlled
background and beads that are small, sparse and embedded in an occluding fibre
network.

\subsection{Segmentation from Few Annotated Images}

Training on a handful of annotated micrographs is standard in microscopy, and the
countermeasures are well established: transfer learning from large natural-image
corpora such as ImageNet \cite{deng2009imagenet} or COCO \cite{lin2014microsoft},
and aggressive geometric and photometric augmentation \cite{shorten2019survey}.
Microscopy is more forgiving than most domains here, since flips and rotations are
genuinely label-preserving, a micrograph having no canonical orientation.

Augmentation also introduces a hazard that is easy to overlook. If augmented
copies are generated before the data are partitioned, transformed versions of the
same source image can appear in both the training and the test set, and the test
set then measures memorisation rather than generalisation. The same concern
applies to any hierarchical dataset in which several images derive from one
physical specimen, a point returned to in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.



\section{Evaluation of Instance Segmentation}

Detection quality is conventionally summarised by an average precision computed
over intersection-over-union thresholds, following the COCO protocol
\cite{lin2014microsoft}. For microscopy, metrics that penalise merges and splits
explicitly are more informative: the aggregated Jaccard index
\cite{kumar2017dataset} accumulates intersection and union over matched objects
and charges the unmatched ones to the denominator, while panoptic quality
\cite{kirillov2019panoptic} factors performance into a recognition and a
segmentation term. Since the laboratory endpoint is a count and a size
distribution, these are reported alongside the counting error and a direct
comparison of the predicted and manual size distributions.

\section{Research Gap}

Three gaps emerge from the work reviewed above.

First, the image-analysis tooling available to the electrospinning community is
aimed at the fibre network, that is at diameter, orientation and porosity, rather
than at discrete defects. DiameterJ and comparable tools do not count beads, so
the quantification of bead defects is still performed by hand.

Second, where deep segmentation has been applied to fibrous microscopy it has
usually been semantic, reporting pixel-level overlap. For a task whose output is a
count this is the wrong figure of merit, since it does not distinguish a model
that separates two touching beads from one that merges them, although only the
former counts correctly. Studies reporting counting error against a manual ground
truth, and comparing instance-level with semantic-level modelling on the same
images, are scarce.

Third, and most consequential for the credibility of any result in this area,
evaluation protocols are frequently not matched to the structure of the data.
Small microscopy datasets are hierarchical, with many images per specimen and many
augmented copies per image, and a partition that ignores this allows the same
specimen, or the same source image, to appear on both sides of the split, leaving
reported scores optimistic by an unknown margin.

This thesis addresses these gaps by treating bead quantification as an instance
segmentation problem, by evaluating it with the counting and sizing errors that
determine laboratory usefulness alongside conventional segmentation metrics, and
by adopting a specimen-wise protocol whose effect relative to the naive
alternative is measured rather than assumed.

\section{Summary}

Bead defects are the standard diagnostic of a misconfigured electrospinning process
and are currently quantified by hand, since the existing automated tools target the
fibre network instead. Classical thresholding fails on these micrographs because
beads and fibres are not separable by intensity. Encoder--decoder networks segment
the bead class well but merge touching instances, thus corrupting precisely the
quantity of interest. Region-based instance segmentation separates instances by
construction and is therefore the approach adopted here. The following chapter sets
out the dataset, the calibration and the evaluation protocol in detail.
