\chapter{INTRODUCTION}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Introduction}

Electrospinning is a fabrication technique in which a high electric field applied
between a spinneret and a grounded collector draws a polymer solution into fibres
with diameters ranging from tens of nanometres to a few micrometres. The non-woven
membranes obtained combine a very high surface-to-volume ratio with an interconnected
pore structure, which explains their use in air and water filtration, in wound
dressings, in drug delivery and as scaffolds for tissue engineering.

The process, however, is not uniformly well behaved. When the electrostatic
stretching force is not sufficient to overcome the surface tension of the jet, the
solution collapses locally into droplets which solidify as ellipsoidal swellings
along the fibre. These \emph{beads}, first characterised systematically by
Fong, Chun and Reneker \cite{fong1999beaded}, are commonly taken as an indicator
that the spinning parameters lie outside their usable window. They reduce the
effective surface area of the membrane, disturb its pore-size distribution and
introduce stress concentrators that weaken it mechanically, so counting and
measuring them is a routine step whenever a recipe is being tuned.

That step is currently performed manually. The operator loads each scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) image into ImageJ \cite{schindelin2012fiji},
identifies the beads by visual inspection and outlines them one at a time. A
single image of the dataset used here contains up to 152 beads, each traced
individually. The procedure is slow and does not scale to the number of
images a parameter study produces, and since the boundary between a large
bead and a locally thick fibre segment is a matter of judgement, the resulting
annotation is difficult to reproduce between operators or even between sessions.

\section{Background and Motivation}

The automation of this measurement is an image segmentation problem, although not
among the simpler ones. Three properties of SEM images of nanofibre membranes make
the task harder than the generic case. The background is dense and
high-contrast, the objects of interest touch and overlap, and the data are
scarce and acquired at several magnifications.

The beads sit on top of a dense background of overlapping fibres. The fibres are
bright, elongated and cross each other at every angle, so a simple intensity
threshold cannot separate beads from background, as both occupy similar grey
levels while the texture energy of the fibre mesh is comparable to that of a bead
surface.

The objects of interest touch and overlap. Beads frequently occur in clusters
along the same fibre, and a method producing only a foreground mask merges such a
cluster into a single connected component. Since the quantities required are a
\emph{count} and a \emph{size distribution}, merging two beads into one is not a
small error, because it lowers the reported count and introduces into the size
histogram a large bead that does not exist. It is this that makes the task an
instance segmentation problem rather than a semantic one.

The data are scarce and acquired at several magnifications. The dataset contains
11 annotated images drawn from four specimens, each imaged at $500\times$,
$1000\times$ and $3000\times$. A bead measuring approximately 14 pixels across at
$500\times$ measures approximately 76 pixels across at $3000\times$, so the same
physical object appears with a sixfold difference in apparent size. A model
trained on such data must be scale-robust, while the evaluation protocol has to
take into account that the four specimens, and not the eleven images, are the
independent units of the experiment.

The first two difficulties are addressed directly by modern instance segmentation
architectures. Mask R-CNN \cite{he2017mask} detects each object separately and
predicts a mask inside its own region proposal, so touching beads remain separate
instances by construction. The third, the limited amount of data and the manner in
which it is evaluated, determines whether the reported numbers can be trusted, and
is given explicit attention throughout this thesis.

\section{Problem Statement}

This thesis addresses the automated detection, delineation and quantification of
beads in SEM images of electrospun nanofibre membranes. The work is
organised around four research questions. The first asks whether a deep instance
segmentation model is able to delineate individual beads in SEM images of
nanofibre membranes with an accuracy sufficient to replace manual annotation, when
only 11 annotated images from four specimens are available. The second asks
whether instance-level modelling offers a measurable advantage over semantic
segmentation in this task, in particular in the separation of touching beads and
consequently in the accuracy of the reported bead count. The third asks how a deep
model compares with the classical image-processing pipeline, consisting of
thresholding followed by watershed splitting, which represents current laboratory
practice. The fourth asks whether the derived physical quantities, namely bead
count, areal density and size distribution expressed in micrometres, agree with
those obtained from the manual annotation closely enough to be used in process
optimisation.

The scope is restricted to beads in images of a single material
system, imaged on one instrument. Fibre diameter measurement, three-dimensional
structure and the relation between spinning parameters and bead formation are
outside it, although they are discussed as possible extensions in
Section~\ref{sec:futurework}.

\section{Research Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

Five objectives are pursued, each with the criterion by which it is judged to
have been met.

The first is to characterise and calibrate the dataset, establishing what the
available data contain and placing every measurement on a physical scale. This
includes recovering the pixel size of each image from its burned-in scale bar and
verifying that calibration against the bead size distribution. It is met if the
median bead diameter agrees to within 20\% across $500\times$, $1000\times$ and
$3000\times$.

The second is a leakage-free evaluation protocol, one in which nothing about the
images a model is tested on is present in those it was trained on. This means
defining a partitioning scheme appropriate to a dataset whose 11 images derive
from only 4 specimens, and
demonstrating quantitatively why a random split over images or over the augmented
copies overstates performance. It is met by a reported gap between the naive and
the specimen-wise protocol obtained with the same model.

The third is instance segmentation of the beads themselves, training Mask R-CNN
to delineate individual beads under leave-one-specimen-out cross-validation. It is
met at $\mathrm{AP}_{50} \geq 0.30$ averaged over the four folds, which is what a
standard region-based detector achieves on a published benchmark of comparable
object size.

This replaces the $\mathrm{AP}_{50} \geq 0.70$ recorded when the work was
    proposed, and the grounds are given here because a criterion revised after the
    results are known is worth nothing unless the grounds are independent of those
    results. The original figure was set before the small-object literature of
    Section~\ref{sec:small_objects} was surveyed, and that survey shows 0.70 to lie
    above what any published detector reaches at this object size. On AI-TOD-v2,
    whose objects average 12.7 pixels against the 14.3-pixel median here, a
    standard Faster R-CNN reaches $\mathrm{AP}_{50} = 0.299$ and the method
    introduced in that paper 0.574 \cite{xu2022tiny}. The revised criterion is the
    first of these, rounded, and it is stated in full awareness that
    Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports 0.298, two thousandths below it.

    One qualification belongs with that number, and it would soften the result.
    The AI-TOD-v2 figure is a \emph{box} average precision while the 0.298 here is
    a \emph{mask} average precision, the stricter of the two. Measured on boxes,
    as the reference is, the detection-only methods of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} do
    clear the criterion, at 0.305 and 0.315. It is nevertheless kept at the mask
    level, where it is not met, because the objective stated here is instance
    segmentation rather than detection.

The fourth is a comparison against five alternatives, quantifying what
instance-level modelling gains and which component is responsible. Mask R-CNN is
compared against the same detector with its mask branch removed, that is Faster
R-CNN \cite{ren2015faster}; against two one-stage detectors of the YOLO family
\cite{jocher2023ultralytics}, a YOLOv8 segmentation model and a YOLOv5u detector;
against a U-Net \cite{ronneberger2015unet} semantic baseline with
connected-component labelling; and against an Otsu \cite{otsu1979threshold} and
watershed \cite{vincent1991watersheds} pipeline representing current practice. It
is met by a clear ranking on counting error, with the merge behaviour of each
method reported explicitly.

The fifth is physical quantification, reporting bead count, areal density
(beads\,mm$^{-2}$) and the size distribution in micrometres, and comparing each
against the manual ground truth. It is met by a counting error together with a
distributional comparison of predicted versus manual bead diameters.

\section{Contributions}

This work makes four contributions. The first is a characterised and physically
calibrated dataset of 606 manually delineated beads across 11 SEM images of four
electrospun specimens, with per-magnification pixel sizes recovered from the
instrument's own scale bars and validated for internal consistency. The second is
an evaluation protocol for this class of small, hierarchically structured
microscopy datasets, in which the specimen rather than the image is the unit of
partitioning, together with a measurement of what a naive split actually costs on
a dataset of this shape. The third is a controlled comparison of six methods on
the same images under the same protocol, evaluated both by segmentation metrics
and by the counting and sizing errors that determine whether a method is usable in
the laboratory. The fourth is an open implementation covering annotation
conversion, scale calibration, training, evaluation and the reporting of physical
quantities.

\section{Thesis Organization}

Chapter~2 reviews electrospinning and bead formation, classical image
analysis for fibrous microscopy and deep segmentation architectures, and
identifies the gap this thesis addresses. Chapter~3 presents the
methodology: the dataset, the scale calibration, the specimen-wise evaluation
protocol, the six methods and the metrics. Chapter~4 describes the
implementation, Chapter~5 the results, and Chapter~6 the
conclusions and future work.
